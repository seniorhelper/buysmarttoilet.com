Upflush toilets for basements: will one work for you?

By Zach Wennstedt · Updated November 10, 2026 · Prices checked November 10, 2026

An upflush toilet works when you can't put a drain under the floor. It sends waste into a sealed pump unit behind the toilet, which grinds it and pumps it through a small pipe up to your existing drain stack. It works if your run to the stack is within the pump's limits, you have a dedicated outlet, and you can vent it. It's the wrong choice if a gravity drain is easy to reach.

How it works

A regular toilet drops waste straight down into a 3-inch drain and lets gravity do the rest. An upflush toilet discharges out the back into a sealed macerator box. Blades break down the waste, and a pump pushes it through a much smaller pipe (about 3/4 to 1 inch on most units) that can run up a wall and across a ceiling to the nearest drain stack. Because the pipe is small and pressurized, you don't need to break concrete.

The three measurements that decide it

  1. Vertical lift. How far up the pipe has to go to reach the stack. The ANZZI Nesso ($699) is rated for up to 26 feet vertical.
  2. Horizontal run. How far across. The Nesso is rated for up to 197 feet horizontal. Lift and run trade off against each other, so check the pump chart in the manual for your exact combination.
  3. Power. The pump plugs in. Plan a dedicated GFCI outlet close to the unit.

Measure the route the pipe will actually take, not the straight-line distance, and count every elbow.

It can drain more than the toilet

Most units have extra inlets, so a sink and sometimes a shower in the same room can drain into the same pump. That's how a basement corner becomes a full bathroom. A shower usually needs the toilet raised a few inches or a small step-up so the shower drain can fall into the unit. Check the inlet heights before you frame anything.

The honest downsides

  • Noise. You'll hear the pump for a few seconds after each flush. Quieter than it used to be, still not silent.
  • What goes in. Only toilet paper and waste. Wipes, even "flushable" ones, and hygiene products jam macerators. Put a sign up for guests.
  • Venting. The unit needs a vent like any other fixture. Local plumbing code decides how; ask your local building department.
  • Power outages. No power, no pump. Plan on not using it during an outage.
  • Permits. Adding a bathroom usually needs a plumbing permit. Many inspectors accept macerating systems; some jurisdictions have specific rules. Ask before you buy.

When a gravity drain is the better answer

If your basement already has a rough-in (capped pipes in the floor), or the main drain is close and below the slab, a regular toilet is simpler and has no pump to maintain. An upflush system makes the most sense when the alternative is cutting a trench through concrete.

What to ask a plumber for

Get at least two written quotes that list the unit, the pipe route, venting, the electrical work and the permit separately. That way you can see where the money goes and compare like for like.

Questions people ask

Do upflush toilets smell?

A properly sealed and vented unit shouldn't. Smells usually point to a venting problem, a bad seal on the macerator lid, or something that shouldn't have been flushed.

Do upflush toilets need a vent?

Yes, in most installations. The unit has a vent connection, and local plumbing code decides how it's run. Check with your building department.

How far can an upflush toilet pump?

It depends on the unit. The ANZZI Nesso is rated for up to 26 feet up or 197 feet across. Higher lift reduces the horizontal distance; use the pump chart in the manual.

Can a shower drain into an upflush toilet?

On units with extra inlets, often yes. The shower drain has to sit higher than the inlet so water can fall into it, which may mean a raised shower base.
