Choosing a toilet or bidet seat for an older adult

By Zach Wennstedt · Updated November 10, 2026 · Prices checked November 10, 2026

For most older adults, the two changes that make the biggest difference are seat height and hygiene without twisting. A comfort-height toilet (17 to 19 inches to the top of the seat, the ADA range) makes standing up easier, and a bidet seat with a simple remote handles cleaning without reaching back. Fancy features matter less than big, clear buttons and a seat that won't shift.

Start with height, not features

The ADA standard puts the top of a toilet seat at 17 to 19 inches above the floor (2010 ADA Standards, section 604.4). Many older standard toilets sit lower, around 15 inches. Those two to four inches are the difference between standing up on your own and needing a hand.

A quick way to check: sit on the current toilet. If the knees end up higher than the hips, the seat is too low for comfortable standing. Note that a bidet seat adds a little height of its own, so measure the toilet and seat together.

Good combinations by situation

Living alone, still fairly mobile

A comfort-height toilet with a soft-close seat, plus a manual bidet seat ($149.99) if there's no outlet. Simple, nothing to charge or reset.

Limited reach or shoulder pain

An electric seat with a wireless remote and warm-air dryer, like the Smart Bidet Seat with auto lid ($299). The dryer and the wash do the reaching. Mount the remote where it's visible from the seat.

Heavier user, or a seat that keeps shifting

The XL Comfort seat ($99) is rated to 800 lb and has wide bumpers that stop the side-to-side shift that makes a seat feel unsafe. Check that the toilet itself is firmly set; a rated seat can't fix a rocking bowl.

Replacing the whole toilet anyway

The two-piece ADA comfort-height toilet ($399) with a bidet seat on top, or a one-piece smart toilet if the person is comfortable with a remote. Ask us for the seat height of a specific model before ordering.

Things that sound good but often go unused

  • Many wash modes. Most people settle on one. Simpler is safer for someone with memory changes.
  • App control. Fine for the caregiver, rarely used by the person on the seat.
  • Auto-opening lids. Handy, but they can startle someone who isn't expecting them. Most can be switched off.

Grab bars still matter

No toilet replaces a properly anchored grab bar. The ADA places side grab bars near the toilet; in a home, have a bar installed into studs or with rated anchors next to the toilet. A towel bar is not a grab bar.

Questions people ask

What height toilet is best for seniors?

A comfort-height toilet that puts the top of the seat between 17 and 19 inches above the floor, the range in the 2010 ADA Standards. Shorter adults sometimes prefer the lower end; taller adults the higher end. A bidet seat adds a small amount of height.

Are bidet seats good for elderly people?

Often, yes. They reduce twisting and reaching for hygiene, and warm water is gentler on skin. Choose one with simple, clearly labeled controls and a seat sensor so it won't spray when nobody is seated.

Is a raised toilet seat or a comfort-height toilet better?

A comfort-height toilet is more stable because there's nothing extra to shift or loosen. A raised seat is cheaper and faster for a short-term need, like recovery after surgery.

What weight can a toilet seat hold?

It varies widely, and many standard seats don't list a rating. Heavy-duty seats list one; the XL Comfort seat we carry is rated to 800 lb. The bowl and how it's anchored to the floor matter too.
