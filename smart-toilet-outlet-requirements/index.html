Smart toilet outlet requirements: check the wall before the cart

By Zach Wennstedt · Published November 17, 2026

Call 1-888-779-2284Send us a photo of the outlet

A smart toilet or electric bidet seat needs a grounded 120-volt outlet within reach of its cord, usually on the wall behind the toilet. In a bathroom that outlet should be GFCI-protected (NEC 210.8(A)(1)) and fed by the bathroom's 20-amp circuit (NEC 210.11(C)(3)). No outlet within reach means an electrician, or a non-electric seat.

In short: look behind your toilet right now. If there is a three-prong outlet with TEST and RESET buttons (or the circuit is GFCI-protected at the panel), most of what we sell will plug straight in. If the nearest outlet is beside the sink, five feet away, plan for a new receptacle before the toilet arrives, because a freight delivery waiting on an electrician is a bad week.

Does every smart toilet need an outlet?

Every model with heat, a warm-water wash, a dryer, a night light or an automatic lid needs power. That is the whole category people mean by "smart toilet." What surprises buyers is how many useful upgrades need no power at all. A manual bidet seat runs on water pressure. A comfort-height toilet is just porcelain and a flush valve. What does the electrical code say about bathroom outlets?

Three parts of the National Electrical Code (NEC) shape what you will find, or need, behind a toilet. Your city or county adopts a specific edition and can amend it, so treat these as the baseline your electrician will start from, not as the final word for your address.

  • GFCI protection, NEC 210.8(A)(1). Receptacles in dwelling-unit bathrooms need ground-fault circuit-interrupter protection. A GFCI cuts power in a fraction of a second when current leaks to ground, which is exactly the failure you worry about with water and electronics in the same fixture.
  • A 20-amp bathroom circuit, NEC 210.11(C)(3). At least one 120-volt, 20-amp branch circuit has to supply the bathroom receptacle outlets. The code allows that circuit to feed other equipment when it serves a single bathroom, which is why an outlet behind the toilet can usually share the bathroom circuit.
  • A receptacle near the basin, NEC 210.52(D). At least one outlet has to be within 3 feet of the outside edge of the sink. That is the outlet most bathrooms have. It is rarely the one a toilet cord can reach.

Older homes often predate these rules. A 1960s bathroom may have one ungrounded outlet in the light fixture, or an outlet on a circuit shared with the bedroom lights. Neither is where a heated seat belongs.

How much power does a smart toilet or bidet seat draw?

Less than people fear, and only in bursts. The heater is the big load. A seat with a small warm-water tank keeps the water warm and draws modest power most of the day. A tankless (instant) heater draws far more for the few seconds it heats water on demand. Bidet King, a bidet retailer, puts peak draw at about 600 watts for tank-type seats and about 1,400 watts for tankless seats, with the high draw lasting only minutes per day. ANZZI's pages for our models do not list wattage, so ask us for the spec sheet of the exact SKU if your circuit is already busy.

Divide watts by 120 volts to get amps. That gives about 5 amps for a tank-type seat at peak and about 11.7 amps for a tankless seat at peak, against a 20-amp bathroom circuit. Run a hair dryer or a space heater on the same circuit at the same moment and the total can pass 20 amps, which is when a breaker trips.

Two practical takeaways. First, a tankless seat on a circuit that also runs the bathroom heater or a hair dryer can trip the breaker on a cold morning, so if you plan to buy one, find out what else is on that circuit. Second, if your lights dim every time the seat heats water, the seat is probably on a lighting circuit. That is worth a call to an electrician whether or not you buy anything from us.

Can I use an extension cord or a power strip?

Not as the permanent answer. Extension cords are made for temporary jobs, and the federal Office of Congressional Workplace Rights says it plainly in the title of its guidance: temporary extension cords "should not be used for permanent wiring." Behind a toilet the cord sits on a floor that gets mopped, splashed and occasionally flooded, and the plug connection is exactly where you don't want water. Follow the manual for your model. If the cord does not reach, you have two honest options: add a receptacle behind the toilet, or buy a non-electric seat like the Minnow ($149.99) and skip the wiring entirely.

Where should the outlet go?

On the wall behind the toilet, a little above the tank or seat line and off to the side the cord exits. Bidet King reports that most seat cords run about 4 feet and exit on the right side (some brands exit left or center). That short length is deliberate: it keeps the cord off the floor. Before you order, do this:

  1. Stand facing the toilet and note which side the model's cord exits (ask us if the spec sheet doesn't say).
  2. Measure from the seat hinge on that side, along the wall, to the nearest outlet. Don't measure in a straight line through the air; the cord follows the wall.
  3. Check that the route doesn't cross the water supply valve or drape across the floor.
  4. If it doesn't reach, tell the electrician the side and height you want, and send them the cord side from the spec sheet.

How do I check what I already have in 10 minutes?

You don't need tools beyond a tape measure and a phone camera.

  1. Find the outlet. Behind the toilet, beside the vanity, inside the vanity cabinet. Photograph each one with the toilet in the frame.
  2. Look for GFCI. An outlet with TEST and RESET buttons is a GFCI receptacle. Press TEST: the power should cut off (a phone charger or night light will show it). Press RESET to restore it. If the bathroom outlet has no buttons, one GFCI upstream may protect it, often in another bathroom or the garage, or a GFCI breaker in the panel may.
  3. Count the prongs. A two-slot outlet with no round ground hole is not suitable for a heated seat. That's an electrician job.
  4. Find the breaker. Turn on a lamp in the outlet, flip breakers until it goes off, and read the number on the handle (15 or 20). Note what else went dark.
  5. Send us the photos. With the outlet photos, the breaker size and your bowl measurement from our round vs elongated fit checker, we can tell you which models will work as-is.

Do I need an electrician, and what should I ask for?

If there's a grounded, GFCI-protected outlet within cord reach, no. If not, adding one receptacle behind a toilet is a small, common job for a licensed electrician, and many jurisdictions require a permit for new wiring, so let the electrician handle that. We don't publish electricians' prices because they vary by city and by how hard it is to get a wire to that wall. Ask for these four things so the quote is comparable:

  • A grounded receptacle on the wall behind the toilet, on the cord-exit side, at a height that keeps the cord off the floor.
  • GFCI protection, either at the receptacle or at the breaker.
  • Fed from the bathroom's 20-amp receptacle circuit, or a new circuit if that one already carries a heater or other heavy loads.
  • A permit and inspection if your jurisdiction requires one.

Schedule the electrician before the toilet ships. Smart toilets arrive by LTL freight to the curb, and an unopened crate in the garage for two weeks is how shipping damage goes unnoticed past the claim window.

What happens when the power goes out?

This varies more than people expect, so check the model. ANZZI says the ENVO Dia has an emergency water-saving flush that works during outages. The ENVO Echo has an emergency battery backup, but the battery isn't supplied, so buy one when you install it, not during the storm. An electric bidet seat on a regular toilet simply stops warming and washing; the toilet underneath keeps flushing normally. A manual seat doesn't notice at all. If you live where outages are common, that alone can tilt the choice toward a bidet seat instead of a full smart toilet.

Is there anything different for an older adult's bathroom?

The outlet rules are the same. The stakes on the cord are higher: a cord across the floor is a trip hazard for someone with a walker, and a remote control that lives on the floor gets lost. Mount the outlet so the cord stays tight to the wall, and keep the remote in a wall holder. Seat height usually matters more than any electronic feature, which is why our guide to choosing a toilet for an older adult starts there. Questions people ask

Does a smart toilet need a dedicated circuit?

Usually not a circuit of its own. Bathroom receptacles are required to be on a 20-amp, 120-volt branch circuit under NEC 210.11(C)(3), and a smart toilet normally plugs into a receptacle on that circuit. If the circuit also feeds a hair dryer or space heater, or it is an older lighting circuit, ask an electrician whether a separate circuit makes sense.

Does the outlet for a bidet seat have to be GFCI?

In a bathroom, yes for new work: NEC 210.8(A)(1) requires GFCI protection for dwelling-unit bathroom receptacles. Protection can come from a GFCI receptacle or a GFCI breaker. If your existing outlet has no TEST and RESET buttons, check the breaker panel or ask an electrician before plugging in a heated seat.

Can I plug a smart toilet into an extension cord?

Do not use an extension cord as the permanent power source. Cords are meant for temporary use, and they sit on a floor that gets wet. If the toilet's cord does not reach an outlet, have a receptacle added behind the toilet, or choose a non-electric model such as a manual bidet seat.

How far can the outlet be from the toilet?

There is no single distance: it depends on the cord length and which side the cord exits. One bidet retailer reports cords of about 4 feet, mostly exiting on the right. Measure the path from the seat hinge to the outlet along the wall before you order, and do not run the cord across the floor.

Will a smart toilet work in a power outage?

It depends on the model. ANZZI says the ENVO Dia has an emergency flush system that works during outages. The ENVO Echo has an emergency battery backup, but the battery is not included. Electric bidet seats simply stop heating and washing, and the toilet under them still flushes normally.
